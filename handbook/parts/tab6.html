Experimental — in active development preview

The curved (conformal) drill-jig wizard is newer and less proven than the flat tool on the first tab. Its two load-bearing stages are confirmed working on live Creo (2026-06-24): STAGE 1 — offset the part’s surface and thicken it into a new body — and STAGE 2 — drill On-Point holes oriented to each fastener’s surface. But the load-bearing correctness question is still open: does pre-selecting the surface truly orient every hole normal to the face, or does Creo sometimes fall back to its own default? The automatic radial chip-relief pattern is probe-gated (it only engages once a one-time recording is captured; until then the tool draws each pocket by hand). Treat this as a preview build: run it, but eyeball every result in Creo before trusting it.

The curved drill-jig wizard (drilljig3d-gui) builds a jig for a part whose drilling face isn’t flat. A flat jig is a plate; a curved jig can’t be. So instead of extruding a box, this tool builds a conformal blank: it copies the part’s curved face, offsets it, and thickens that into a new solid body that follows the curvature. It then drills each bushing hole normal to that surface (not normal to a flat top), and cuts a symmetric chip-relief pocket on each fastener’s own datum plane. Like the flat tool it is input-first: you make every pick and type every number up front, then the build fires hands-free, and the only thing left to do by hand is draw one small relief rectangle per hole at the end.

This zip is the curved .cmd launcher plus all resources — the PowerShell libs (conformal-blank, fastener-hole, chip-relief, radial-pattern, the shared geometry + wizard framework), the bushing catalogs, and the decision tree. The 3D bushing preview uses WPF (built into Windows), so — unlike the flat tool — there is no WebView2 or three.js to bundle. No network access. Not yet live-verified end-to-end; see the WIP note above.

2. Quick start

  1. Extract the folder anywhere writable (Desktop or Documents — not a locked/protected path).
  2. Open Creo and, in the assembly, activate the drilljig PART (right-click it → Activate).
  3. Double-click drilljig3d-gui.cmd.
  4. Walk the wizard: Welcome → Fasteners → Surface → Conditions → Build → Slots → Done.
The shape of the flow is different from the flat tool
The flat wizard interleaves input and building at each stage. The curved wizard front-loads everything: you pick the fasteners, pick the surface, and answer every question first; then one button (Build everything) builds the blank, rounds the corners, and drills every hole hands-free; then you draw the relief pockets. Fewer trips back and forth to Creo.

4. Insight — the conformal blank

This is the idea that makes a curved jig possible. A flat jig starts from a rectangle you sketch and extrude. A curved jig starts from the part’s own surface: the tool offsets that face (copying its exact curvature), then thickens the copy into a new solid body. The result is a shell that hugs the part, at a constant wall thickness, everywhere along the curve.

Two things are automatic — you never type them
  • Part thickness = bushing length + chip clearance. The blank is thickened to exactly the amount that leaves a full bushing seat plus the material the relief pocket removes. You choose the chip clearance on one card (§6); the thickness follows.
  • Standoff = 0 (flush). The jig sits directly on the part. There is no “float it off the surface” question in this flow.

The thicken flips away from the part by default, so the new material grows outward (into free space) rather than into the part you’re jigging. If a particular part defaults the correct way already, --no-flip turns the flip off.

5. Insight — normal-to-surface drilling (and the open question)

On a flat jig every hole is parallel — they all point straight down through the plate. On a curved jig, “straight down” is different at every hole, because the surface tilts as it curves. Each hole must be drilled along its local surface normal so the bushing sits square and the drilled part hole lands true.

The tool does this by creating each hole On-Point and orienting it to that fastener’s own TOP datum plane — the plane whose normal matches the surface there. Pre-selecting that reference before firing the hole is what (should) make Creo orient the hole normal to the face.

This is the load-bearing open question verifying
Whether the surface pre-select reliably orients every hole normal — versus Creo silently taking its own default direction — is the correctness question the project is still nailing down with a live probe. If you ever see a hole that isn’t square to the face, that’s this. The fallback flag --default-orient drills with Creo’s default On-Point direction (skip the per-hole orientation) if the oriented path misbehaves on your part. Always verify hole orientation in Creo.

6. Insight — symmetric chip-relief, and the radial pattern

Chip relief on a curved jig is a shallow pocket cut over each hole so swarf and the drill can clear. Because the face is curved, the pocket is cut as a symmetric remove-material extrude on each fastener’s own TOP plane — the same plane the hole was oriented to. You draw one rectangle over the hole; the tool cuts a pocket that straddles the plane, depth = 2× the chip clearance (half on each side). The blank was already thickened by the clearance in STAGE 1, so there is material to remove.

The radial / axis pattern — draw one, get all

When the fasteners are uniformly spaced around a cylinder axis (a bolt circle on a barrel-shaped part), drawing a rectangle over every hole is wasteful. The tool can instead have you draw one seed pocket, then pattern it around the axis — Creo rotates each copy so it re-orients to the new normal (a linear pattern can’t do this on a curve; an axis pattern can). Non-uniform layouts are split into as many pattern groups as the angles need, and anything left over falls back to a hand-drawn pocket.

probe-gated The radial pattern only engages once a one-time recording of the axis-pick is captured. Until then — and whenever --no-radial-pattern is passed or the fasteners aren’t uniform — the tool uses the guaranteed path: draw one pocket per hole. Not yet live-verified.

Chip clearance
Stage: Conditions
“Next”

The single sizing input for the whole jig. Chip clearance is how deep each relief pocket cuts (a symmetric pocket = 2× this value), and the blank is thickened by the same amount so there is material to remove. Standard clearance is 0.25″; Tight / custom lets you type a smaller value. The part thickness (= bushing length + clearance) and the flush standoff both follow from this — you are never asked for them.

Enabled when

7
Build the jig (hands-free) commit point
Stage: Build
“Build everything”

The commit point. In one hands-free pass the tool: (1) offsets + thickens the surface into a new body, (2) rounds the blank’s corners (skip with --no-corner-round), and (3) drills every fastener hole normal to the surface. It only pauses if the automatic resolve of a fastener’s planes misses — then it asks you to tree-pick that fastener’s three planes. Don’t touch Creo while it runs. Chip-relief pockets are cut in the next stage.

Enabled when
A surface, a positive thickness, a valid hole diameter, and ≥1 fastener are all set.
What you do
Press Build everything and wait.
Creo pick
Mostly no — only a fallback tree-pick if a fastener’s planes don’t auto-resolve.

8
Chip-relief pockets: draw the first draws in Creo
Stage: Slots
“Open the first pocket sketch”

Arms the chip relief. It reuses the fasteners you already selected (no re-picking): for the first one it pre-selects that fastener’s own TOP datum plane (the plane its hole was drilled normal to) and opens the extrude so the sketch opens right on it — hands-free, no reference planes. You draw one rectangle over the hole and leave the sketch open. If the automatic plane pre-select doesn’t take, the tool asks you to click that plane.

Enabled when
Always (self-skips if chip relief is off, or if there is no blank body / no fasteners).
What you do
Press the button; draw one rectangle over the first hole; leave the sketch open.
Creo pick
Yes — draw the rectangle (and click the plane only if the pre-select misses).
9
Chip-relief pockets: cut + next
Stage: Slots
“Finish this pocket”

Finishes the pocket you just drew: the tool cuts a symmetric remove-material extrude (depth = 2× the chip clearance) straddling the TOP plane, then re-arms the next fastener and re-opens its sketch — so this one step loops over every hole (draw → Finish → draw the next). When the fasteners form a uniform bolt circle and the radial pattern is available, it instead cuts one seed and patterns the rest around the axis (you pick the rotation axis once). A wedged re-arm can’t trap you — a no-progress guard bails out honestly.

Enabled when
Once a pocket sketch is armed.
What you do
Press Finish this pocket; draw the next when it re-arms; repeat until done.
Creo pick
Yes — one rectangle per hole (or one seed + an axis pick for the radial pattern).

  1. Select the fasteners (step 2) — Ctrl-click every fastener component, then verify.
  2. Pick the surface (step 3) — click the curved face the jig follows, then verify.

9. Command-line flags (optional)

Pass these to drilljig3d-gui.cmd to override defaults or skip questions:

FlagEffect
--default-orientDrill with Creo’s default On-Point direction (skip the per-hole normal-to-surface orientation). The fallback if oriented drilling misbehaves on your part.
--no-relief (alias --no-slots)Disable chip relief entirely — no thicken bump, no pockets.
--relief-depth N (alias --slot-depth N)Chip-relief depth in inches (default 0.25). The pocket is cut 2N deep (N each side of the plane); prefills the chip-clearance card.
--no-corner-roundSkip the Build-stage auto corner-rounding of the blank.
--corner-radius NCorner-round fillet radius in inches (default 0.25).
--no-flipDo not flip the thicken side. The thicken flips by default so the blank grows away from the part; use this only if a part defaults the correct way.
--no-radial-patternDisable the automatic radial/axis chip-relief pattern; draw every pocket by hand (the guaranteed path).
