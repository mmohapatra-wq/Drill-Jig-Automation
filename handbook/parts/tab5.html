The wizard is tuned for the common case — a flat, rectangular guide plate on an orthogrid of holes. But the geometry it builds is ordinary Creo: parametric planes, an extruded plate, drilled guide holes, and chip-relief slots. That means once the wizard has done the heavy lifting, a few manual tweaks in the sketcher unlock jigs that are far from rectangular — L-shapes, stepped multi-region plates, and small wrap-around corner jigs. The examples below are real drill-jigs built for flight-hardware thrust-structure work; each one started in drilljig-gui and was finished with light hand-editing.

Why this tab exists
The point isn’t to document a fixed recipe — it’s to widen the aperture on what’s possible. If the part you’re jigging isn’t a clean rectangle, don’t walk away: run the wizard to get the plate, holes, and slots roughly right, then adjust the plate outline or split the hole regions by hand. Aft Module Thrust Structure — 150-110-0030-101

A drill-jig for the ASSY THRUST STRUCTURE AFT MODULE TS. The part isn’t a single rectangular pad: the guide holes fall into two offset regions on different pitches, so the plate was drawn as a stepped L to hug both hole clusters while clearing the surrounding structure. The wizard produced the plate, the seated bushing holes, and the chip-relief slots along the bottom edge; the non-rectangular outline is a single manual edit to the plate sketch.

The one adjustment that made it possible
The wizard drew a rectangle over the full hole envelope; the operator then edited that rectangle into the stepped L outline in the open sketcher (removing the empty corner the structure needed clear). Everything downstream — holes, slots, the 3D preview — regenerated on the new outline with no further changes.

FWD Demo BUDS Corner — 150-510-9010-001

A compact corner jig for the TS FWD Demo BUDS Corner. Instead of a flat pad, this one wraps a lip over the edge of the part so it self-locates on a corner — a small manual flange added to the wizard’s plate. It carries an engraved +X1 orientation datum and a “DO NOT USE FOR FLIGHT” callout so it can never be mistaken for flight hardware, plus the jig’s own part number 004-986-4656-001.

The one adjustment that made it possible
Starting from the wizard’s flat plate, the operator added a short flange so the jig hooks over the part’s corner and self-registers — no separate fixture needed. Engraved text (orientation datum, flight-safety note, part number) was added as cosmetic features afterward.

Curved Drill Jig — MK1 Aft Structure — 030-101-0002-001

The first jig in this handbook that leaves the flat wizard behind. This plate is not flat — it follows the conformal curvature of the aft-structure skin, so it was started in drilljig3d-gui rather than the flat wizard. Partway along its length the plate steps up in height to hug a shoulder in the part, and the comb of chip-relief slots runs the full length between the two guide-hole columns.

The one adjustment that made it possible
Unlike the flat examples, the starting geometry here is a conformal surface offset — the wizard’s curved path wraps the plate to the skin before a single hole is placed. The only manual edit in the sketcher was the stepped height change to clear the shoulder; everything else came off the curved path as-generated.

Star Stiffener — 030-101-0270-001

Where three stiffener arms radiate from a common node, the drilling all happens at a single Y-junction — and every hole leans in toward the vertex. This jig is a wide chevron that bridges two of the arms at once, carrying two angled hole regions that converge on the point where the arms meet. It came off the wizard as a rough V and needed only a little hand-work in the sketcher to square the guide columns to each arm’s true axis.

Slots are a rigidity dial — tune them by hand
The chip-relief slots are parametric sketch features, so once the wizard lays them down you can widen, narrow, or shorten them right in the Creo sketcher. On a small chevron like this the web of material left between slots is what actually carries load — it is what keeps the plate from flexing while you lean on the drill. To stiffen the plate: narrow each slot so more web remains beside it, trim the slot depth so the slot cuts less deep into the plate, or delete a slot or two entirely and leave fewer, wider webs between the guide-hole columns. Every one of those moves trades a little chip clearance for a stiffer plate that resists flex and holds each hole’s true position through the cut. Conversely, if chips are packing and binding the bit, widen or deepen a slot — but only as far as the web between slots can still carry the drilling load without the plate springing. Treat slot width, depth, and web spacing as a deliberate rigidity-versus-clearance tradeoff you control after the wizard runs, not a fixed part of the geometry.
Try it on your part
If your job looks more like these than like a plain rectangle, still start in drilljig-gui — let it place the holes, bushings, and slots — then reshape the plate or add a locating feature by hand. The wizard does the tedious layout math; you keep full control of the outline.
